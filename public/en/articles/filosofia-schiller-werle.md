# The Relationship Between Schiller's Aesthetic Philosophy and Samir Yazbek's Play O Fingidor (The Pretender)

*<span id="tempo-leitura"></span>. 10/12/26.*

---
In the last few days, I have motivated myself to research German Romanticism, especially through the videos of a USP professor, the true *Weltseele* of Brazilian Hegelianism, Marco Aurélio Werle. One video in particular excited me: *Goethe e a Filosofia: Teatro e Vida como Formação Cultural* (Goethe and Philosophy: Theatre and Life as Cultural Formation). At one point, he discusses the work of the Romantic philosopher Schiller, *Letters Upon the Aesthetic Education of Man*. Werle indirectly mentions it and soon presents the following quotation: "To arrive at a solution even in the political problem, the road of aesthetics must be pursued, because it is through beauty that we arrive at freedom." When the professor said that, the Kantian core of my soul was shaken. Although my transcendental ethics are sustainable, they are incomplete and abstract because they proceed only from a strict Kantian code. The aesthetic is an ethical component that incorporates human experiences into the categorical scope. Ethics does not have to proceed only through rationalism, but also through aesthetic knowledge. Therefore, I came to a conclusion that was necessary in itself: my lifestyle should not be based only on philosophical and literary lessons; I must also educate myself through the aesthetic, to apprehend the Good-in-itself and the Beautiful.

Firstly, drawing on the previous conclusion, I would like to establish a parallel with Goethe's *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* (the work discussed in the aforementioned video), whose narrative follows the protagonist, Wilhelm, as he defies the expectations of his time by pursuing a career in the performing arts as a means of attaining individual freedom and personal fulfilment. In this regard, I realised that committing myself to theatrical life was an absolute necessity for me. Theatre is an absolute phenomenon of Spirit; indeed, art itself is. In my artistic slumber, I had failed to recognise the function of art and the way in which it reveals Truth in a sensuous form. Following this new understanding and this necessarily new mode of being, I decided to enter this world. And it is with great happiness that I tell you that my introduction to it came through the play *O Fingidor* (*The Pretender*), by Samir Yazbek.

In conclusion, with these revelations, I searched for the theatrical programme of São José dos Campos (my city). In particular, most of the options seemed very boring, either because they were comedies or because they were nothing. However, one of them caught my attention: a play about Fernando Pessoa (a Portuguese writer and poet). Although I have a shameful lack of knowledge about the writer, any subject related to culture is agreeable to me. I had the privilege of seeing it on Friday, the 9th of October, 2026, and even more agreeable was the company of a dear friend of mine, a scientific researcher. The play lasted, unfortunately, only an hour and a half, and it was one of the best moments of my year. The play entered and romanticised my very self; the performances and the drama were exciting for me. With this, I realised that Schiller's phrase really has a touch of reason: appreciating the fine arts makes us more alive, empathetic and rational.

At the end, as the play came to a close, I had a very brief conversation with the brilliant mind behind it, Samir Yazbek. It makes me happy to talk to artistic minds such as his. After the event, I sought to learn more about the playwright and watched some of his interviews, wishing to get to know more of his work, such as the plays *Fogo-Fátuo* (*Will-o'-the-Wisp*), *O Eterno Retorno* (*The Eternal Return*) and *Uma Família à Procura de um Ator* (*A Family in Search of an Actor*). In addition, I also became interested in studying playwriting. Although I do not wish to pursue a career in this field, it is always good to fill oneself with the passions of the artistic world. If Kant said *Sapere Aude*, I would add: *Sentire Aude!*
